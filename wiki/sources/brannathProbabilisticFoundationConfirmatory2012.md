# Probabilistic foundation of confirmatory adaptive designs

Competence Center for Clinical Trials, University of Bremen, and Medical University of Vienna. A Theory and Methods paper answering the objection, raised most sharply by Liu, Proschan and Pledger 2002, that adaptive designs with unspecified adaptation rules may not even live on a probability space, let alone control the type I error [brannathProbabilisticFoundationConfirmatory2012].

## Summary
A confirmatory adaptive design fixes the first stage, then lets the investigator pick the second-stage design d = γ(x) from a design space D after seeing interim data x, with γ unknown. Two principles are supposed to control the type I error: the conditional type I error principle (Proschan and Hunsberger 1995; Müller and Schäfer 2001, 2004), which prespecifies a conditional error function A with ∫A dG = α and demands P(R_d | X = x) ≤ A(x) for the chosen rejection region (eq. 2.1); and the conditional invariance principle (Brannath, König and Bauer 2007), whose special case is the combination test, demanding a second-stage p-value that is conditionally stochastically larger than uniform (eq. 2.2). Both are stated through conditional probabilities, which exist only up to null sets. The paper's counterexample (Sec. 3) has a bivariate-normal first and second stage, uncorrelated under design d_0 and positively correlated under d_1, with A ≡ α and R_d = {Y > z_α}: choosing the version of P(R_{d_1} | X = x) that has been modified on a null set containing the observed x makes (2.1) and (2.2) hold formally, yet the rule γ = d_0 for x ≤ z_α and d_1 for x > z_α has rejection probability (1 − α)α + P_{d_1}(X > z_α, Y > z_α) > α by Slepian's inequality. The same move invalidates the combination test. Liu, Proschan and Pledger avoid this by assuming D countable and the stronger condition (4.1) holding for almost all x in γ^{−1}(d), which excludes legitimate practice such as increasing the sample size after a promising interim result when the ordinary z-test has smaller conditional error only at the observed x. The paper's solution is to require a regression model: the conditional distribution F_{x,d} of the second-stage data given interim data x and design d must be specified for every x and d by a structural model, as in regression analysis where covariates are parameters rather than random variables. This "regression independence" is stronger than stochastic independence (Sec. 5.2) and pins down a unique canonical version. Under a correct regression model the outcomes of any adaptive trial, with uncountable D and non-measurable γ, can be embedded in a probability space on which the rejection region is contained in a set of probability α (Sec. 6, Appendix), which is "a sufficiently rigorous justification" and shows that adaptive designs "require no more assumptions than other standard statistical test designs".

## Main results
- **The two principles (Sec. 2, eqs 2.1–2.2).** Conditional type I error principle: prespecify measurable A : R^n → [0, 1] with ∫ A(x) dG(x) = α and choose for d = γ(x) a rejection region R_d with P(R_{γ(x)} | X = x) ≤ A(x). Conditional invariance principle: prespecify a combination test decision ψ(x, u), non-increasing in u, with ∫∫ ψ(x, u) du dG(x) = α, and choose a second-stage p-value p_d with P(p_{γ(x)} ≤ u | X = x) ≤ u for all u (eq. 2.2). Both are expected to give P(R_γ) ≤ α by integrating over x, and both require a choice of version of the conditional probability.
- **Counterexample (Sec. 3).** First-stage z-score X and second-stage z-score Y bivariate normal; D = {d_0, d_1} with correlation 0 under d_0 and ρ_{d_1} > 0 under d_1 (a switch from a short-term to a long-term endpoint whose interim patients contribute to the second stage); A ≡ α, R_d = {Y > z_α}. Versions of P(R_{d_1} | X = x) include π(x) = α on any null set N and 1 − Φ((z_α − xρ)/√(1 − ρ²)) off it; taking N ∋ x after observing x makes (2.1) hold, yet with γ(x) = 1{x ≤ z_α}d_0 + 1{x > z_α}d_1 the rejection probability is (1 − α)α + P_{d_1}(X > z_α, Y > z_α) > (1 − α)α + α² = α by Slepian's inequality. The same version trick on the conditional distribution function of Y given X breaks the combination test with p_d = 1 − Φ(Y). A referee's objection that P(R_{d_1} | X = x) = α is "not a valid mathematical statement" is accepted as the point: without a prespecified or canonical version there is nothing against which the faulty use can be detected, by the statistician or a regulator. Müller and Schäfer 2004 noted the need for a prespecified version but did not meet it. Bahadur and Bickel 1968 and Perlman and Wichura 1975 treat the analogous substitution problem for conventional conditional tests with a known overall space, which does not transfer to an unknown adaptation rule.
- **Liu, Proschan and Pledger's solution and its cost (Sec. 4, eq. 4.1).** With D countable and P(R_d | X = x′) ≤ A(x′) for almost all x′ ∈ γ^{−1}(d) whenever P(γ^{−1}(d)) > 0, every version works. But γ^{−1}(d) is unknown, and requiring the bound for almost all x′ excludes the important case of increasing the sample size at a promising interim result (Chen, DeMets and Lan 2004; Brannath, König and Bauer 2007; Gao, Ware and Mehta 2008; Mehta and Pocock 2011), where the ordinary z-test's conditional error is below A(x) at the observed x but not at almost all x′.
- **Regression models (Sec. 5).** Y = (x + z)1{x = z} + ε shows that the conditional law of Y given covariates depends on whether P(X = Z) is zero or positive, so a single probability model for (Y, X, Z) cannot determine the regression model; a regression model specifies F_{x,z} for every (x, z) and so implicitly a probability model for every covariate distribution. Regression independence (the same conditional law for all (x, z)) is stronger than stochastic independence and can be "invisible" to it. The counterexample's deficiency is that bivariate normality with known ρ_d is too weak; the regression model Y = ρ_d x + √(1 − ρ_d²) ε for all x and d fixes F_{x,d} and then (2.1) and (2.2) visibly fail for R_d = {Y > z_α}. Post-hoc covariate transformations in regression analysis raise the same existence question (Sec. 5.3).
- **Embedding theorem for the conditional error principle (Sec. 6.1, Appendix A.1–A.2).** Assume G known under H_0 and the regression model F_{x,d} for all x ∈ R^n, d ∈ D; prespecify A with ∫A dG = α; at the observed x choose R_{x,d} with π_{x,d} = ∫_{R_{x,d}} dF_{x,d} ≤ A(x) (specified only at the observed x, unspecified elsewhere). If the conditional levels are exhausted, π_{x,γ(x)} = A(x) for all x, then Ω = {(x, y)} with the σ-algebra {Ω, R_γ, Ω \ R_γ, ∅} and probabilities 1, α, 1 − α, 0 is a probability space in which P(R_γ) = α, for arbitrary γ. If π_{x,γ(x)} ≤ A(x) and γ is non-measurable, x ↦ π_{x,γ(x)} may fail to be Borel, so one enlarges each R_{x,d} by a biased coin with success probability b_{x,d} = (A(x) − π_{x,d})/(1 − π_{x,d}) (A.1), after which the levels are exhausted and the original rejection region is contained in a set of probability α. Remarks: X is made measurable by enriching the σ-algebra with P({X ∈ E} ∩ R_γ) = ∫_E A dG (2); the space depends on γ and is unknown, but only its existence is needed (3), and A.2 gives an alternative construction on triples (x, d, y_d) that does not depend on γ at all; external information W that is regression independent of the first stage is handled by W × Ω with the power set on W, provided A does not depend on it (4, A.3). The same construction justifies F- and t-tests after unscheduled, response-independent covariate transformations (1).
- **Embedding for the conditional invariance principle (Sec. 6.2, Appendix A.4).** Require for every x and d a p-value p_{x,d} with conditional distribution function H_{x,d}(u) ≤ u for all u and all x (eq. 6.1), stronger than "stochastically larger than uniform" which fixes H only up to null sets. If H_{x,d}(u) = u everywhere, P(X ∈ E, p_γ ≤ u) = u ∫_E dG extends to a measure on σ(X, p_γ) under which p_γ is uniform and independent of X, so E[ψ(X, p_γ)] = α. If H can be strictly below u and is continuous, q = H_{x,γ(x)}(p_γ) is exactly uniform and q ≤ p_γ pointwise, so rejection with ψ(X, p_γ) implies rejection with ψ(X, q), which is level α. With jumps, an auxiliary uniform U regression independent of the trial produces a uniform q ≤ p_γ (A.4). Overall p-values from a prespecified nested family of conditional error functions A_u are justified the same way.
- **Non-measurable sample-size rule revisited (Sec. 6.3).** Liu, Proschan and Pledger's example with γ(x) = n on a partition of (0, 1] into non-measurable sets E_n and second-stage p-value p_n uniform and independent of X: the rejection region ∪_n (E_n × {p_n ≤ 0.05}) is not shown non-measurable merely because the E_n are (unions of non-measurable sets can be measurable, (0, 1] itself being one), and on σ(X, p_γ) with P(X ∈ E, p_γ ≤ u) = u·|E| the rejection probability is exactly 0.05.
- **Discussion (Sec. 7).** Two conclusions: a regression model is needed and is justified under minimal assumptions when the two stages come from independent cohorts; under it no countability or measurability assumption on D or γ is needed. Violating the rejection rule on a non-prespecified null set produces arbitrary inflation, in conventional tests as well, but adaptive designs can only avoid it through a unique canonical version implied by the chosen design "and hence used by everyone". Nuisance parameters (residual variance, baseline hazard) and one-sided composite nulls are handled by pivotal statistics or by taking the maximum conditional error over the null, or applying the principles simultaneously for all null parameter values (Müller and Schäfer 2004; Timmesfeld, Schäfer and Müller 2007; Gutjahr, Brannath and Bauer 2011). Type I error control is only one requirement; in practice the design space will be restricted for interpretability and power planning.

## Setting and assumptions
- Null: H_0 with known first-stage distribution G of the interim statistic X ∈ R^n; composite nulls with nuisance parameters are discussed in Sec. 7 only.
- Two stages (multi-stage designs via nested or recursive constructions are mentioned, not treated); the second-stage design d ∈ D, with D arbitrary and the adaptation rule γ : R^n → D unknown and possibly non-measurable; external information allowed if regression independent of the first stage.
- Key assumption: a regression model giving the conditional distribution F_{x,d} of the second-stage data for every interim value x and design d, not merely a joint law; automatically justified when the stages come from independent patient cohorts, and a substantive assumption when patients contribute to both stages (survival trials, endpoint switches).
- No optimality or power considerations; no sequential e-values, though the conditional error function A is one in disguise (see [brannathAnytimevalidTestingEvalues2026a](brannathAnytimevalidTestingEvalues2026a.md)).
