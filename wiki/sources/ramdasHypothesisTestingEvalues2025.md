# Hypothesis testing with e-values

## Summary
A 16-chapter monograph that treats the e-value, a nonnegative statistic with expectation at most one under the null, as the basic object of a theory of statistical evidence [ramdasHypothesisTestingEvalues2025]. Part I (Chapters 1–4) fixes definitions and conventions, derives validity under the null (Markov's inequality, calibrators, randomized tests, admissibility), defines efficiency under the alternative (powered e-variables, e-power, log-optimality, mixture and plug-in methods), and shows that e-values are exactly what is needed for post-hoc and data-dependent decisions. Part II (Chapters 5–9) develops the constructive core: universal inference for arbitrary composite nulls, the numeraire e-variable and its strong duality with the reverse information projection (existence of a unique log-optimal e-variable with no assumptions), e-processes and sequential anytime-valid inference via Ville's inequality and testing by betting, merging of dependent, independent and sequential e-values, and the e-BH procedure with the theorem that every FDR-controlling procedure is e-BH applied to some compound e-values. Part III (Chapters 10–16) covers approximate and asymptotic e-values, optimal-transport proofs that admissible e-merging is weighted averaging and admissible p-merging goes through calibrators, e-confidence intervals and the e-BY procedure, a duality between e- and p-variables, improved thresholds under shape constraints, and e-statistics for risk measures. The preface states the thesis: powered tests exist iff powered e-variables exist; all risk-controlled decisions with more than two actions or data-dependent losses threshold e-values; universal inference is the only known test for several irregular problems; the numeraire is a well-defined log-optimal e-value for any composite null; and anytime-valid and arbitrary-dependence multiple testing methods must be e-value based [ramdasHypothesisTestingEvalues2025, Preface].

## Main results
All claims are from [ramdasHypothesisTestingEvalues2025]; numbering follows the author-final PDF.

1. **E-variable and p-variable (Definition 1.2).** An e-variable for a null P is a [0,∞]-valued E with E_P[E] ≤ 1 for all P ∈ P; exact if equality holds. A p-variable satisfies P(P ≤ α) ≤ α. Averages of e-variables and products of independent e-variables are e-variables (Fact 1.3). For a simple null, admissible e-variables are exactly exact ones, i.e. likelihood ratios dS/dP (Proposition 2.12).
2. **Markov and its refinements (Proposition 2.1, Theorem 2.9, Theorem 5.6).** P(E ≥ 1/α) ≤ α, so 1/E is a p-variable and 1{E ≥ 1/α} is a level-α test. Randomized Markov: P(E ≥ U/α) ≤ α for U uniform and independent, so U/E is a strictly smaller p-variable (Corollary 2.10). Exchangeable Markov: for exchangeable e-variables, P(∃n : (1/n)Σ_{i≤n} Z_i ≥ 1/α) ≤ α, with a uniformly randomized version (Theorem 5.7).
3. **Calibrators (Definition 2.3, Propositions 2.4, 2.5, 2.7).** The only admissible e-to-p calibrator is e ↦ min(1, 1/e). A decreasing f with f = 0 on (1,∞) is a p-to-e calibrator iff ∫_0^1 f ≤ 1, admissible iff f is left-continuous, f(0) = ∞ and ∫_0^1 f = 1; examples κp^{κ−1}, (1−p+p log p)/(p(−log p)²), 2(1−p), p^{−1/2}−1, −log p. Every e-variable on an atomless space is a calibrator applied to an exact p-variable. The log-optimal calibrator against Q is f(p) = E_P[dQ/dP | P] when decreasing (Corollary 3.23).
4. **Existence equivalence (Theorem 3.9, Theorem 3.17, Theorem 6.10).** Under a uniform-randomization assumption (U), the following are equivalent: a bounded powered e-variable exists; a powered level-α test exists; a powered binary test exists; a powered p-variable exists. With a common reference measure, a uniformly powered bounded e-variable exists iff d_TV(Conv P, Conv Q) > 0. In general, a powered e-variable for P against Q exists iff Q is outside the bipolar P°°.
5. **E-power and log-optimality (Definitions 3.11, 3.18; Theorems 3.14, 3.20, 3.28).** E-power is E_Q[log E]; it is the asymptotic growth rate of products of iid copies (Proposition 3.12). E_Q[X] > 1 iff E_Q[log(1−λ+λX)] > 0 for some λ, so a powered e-variable can always be made e-powered by shrinking toward 1. For simple P vs Q ≪ P the unique log-optimal e-variable is dQ/dP with e-power KL(Q,P). Any ranking of e-variables satisfying homogeneity and asymptotic consistency axioms is a strictly increasing transform of E_Q[log E].
6. **Mixture and plug-in methods (Section 3.7, Proposition 7.14, Corollary 7.15).** For simple null and composite alternative, ∫Π q(X_i)/p(X_i) ν(dq) and Π q_{i−1}(X_i)/p(X_i) are e-variables (and test martingales) that are asymptotically log-optimal under weak conditions; for concave log-likelihood ratios, L²-consistent plug-ins suffice.
7. **Post-hoc validity (Definition 4.1, Theorem 4.9).** E-variables yield post-hoc valid families of tests, E_P[sup_α ϕ_α/α] ≤ 1, so the level may depend on the data; with more than two decisions or data-dependent loss, every decision rule satisfying the risk constraint is an e-value thresholding rule, and p-value rules fail in general.
8. **Universal inference (Theorem 5.1, Propositions 5.3, 5.4, 7.18).** With data split into D_0, D_1, any alternative q̂_1 fitted on D_1 and the null MLE p̂_0 on D_0, Π_{D_0} q̂_1/p̂_0 is an e-variable with no regularity conditions; averaging over B random splits weakly increases e-power (Jensen). The running-MLE version Π_{i≤t} q̂_{i−1}(X_i)/p̂_t(X_i) is an e-process dominated by a test martingale. The numeraire always dominates the universal inference e-variable (Section 6.5), so the latter is inadmissible.
9. **Numeraire and RIPr (Definitions 6.1, 6.7; Proposition 6.3; Theorems 6.4, 6.13, 6.15).** The numeraire E* is the Q-a.s. positive e-variable with E_Q[E/E*] ≤ 1 for all e-variables E; it always exists, is unique, and is exactly the log-optimal e-variable. The RIPr P* is defined by dP*/dQ = 1/E*; it is a sub-probability measure in the bipolar P°° (the effective null). Strong duality: E_Q[log E*] = sup_E E_Q[log E] = inf_{P ∈ P°°} KL(Q,P) = KL(Q,P*), possibly infinite. The numeraire is the only e-variable that is a likelihood ratio dQ/dP for some P in the effective null equivalent to Q. For finite nulls the RIPr is the KL-closest convex combination (Proposition 6.6).
10. **E-processes and Ville (Definition 7.3, Facts 7.5, 7.6, Propositions 7.8, 7.9).** A test supermartingale is a nonnegative supermartingale with M_0 ≤ 1 under every null P. An e-process is an adapted sequence with E_P[E_τ] ≤ 1 for every stopping time τ, equivalently one dominated by a test (super)martingale for each P. Ville: P(∃t : M_t ≥ 1/α) ≤ α. Every level-α sequential test is a thresholded e-process; e-processes can be optionally continued by multiplying a fresh e-process started at a stopping time. The likelihood ratio process is log-optimal at every Q-a.s. finite stopping time (Theorem 7.11).
11. **Testing by betting and sequential e-values (Algorithms 7.16, 7.17; Definitions 7.19, 7.21; Theorem 7.22).** Any predictable sequence of conditional e-variables yields a test supermartingale wealth process; against a composite null the statistician's wealth is the infimum over separate games, which is the e-process view. For sequential e-values E_t (E_P[E_t | F_{t−1}] ≤ 1), the empirically adaptive e-process Π(1−λ_s+λ_s E_s) with λ_t fitted to past e-values is asymptotically log-optimal within its class and consistent when E_Q[E_1] > 1. Time mixtures Σ_{j≤n} w(j)E^{(j)} turn any sequence of e-variables into an increasing e-process (Proposition 7.23).
12. **Merging e-values (Proposition 8.3, Theorem 8.4, Theorem 8.12).** Under arbitrary dependence the arithmetic mean essentially dominates every symmetric e-merging function, and the admissible e-merging functions are exactly the weighted means Σλ_k e_k + λ_{K+1}; under independence the product is admissible but high-variance; for sequential e-values every se-merging function is dominated by a martingale merging function.
13. **E-BH and universality (Definitions 9.1, 9.8; Theorems 9.11, 9.18, 9.19, 9.21).** Compound e-variables satisfy Σ_{k: P ∈ P_k} E_P[E_k] ≤ K. E-BH rejects the k* largest with k* = max{k : k e_[k]/K ≥ 1/α}. Any self-consistent procedure controls FDR ≤ α under arbitrary dependence, with the post-hoc bound E[sup_α FD_α/(αR_α)] ≤ 1 and K_0/K when inputs are genuine e-variables. Conversely, from any FDR-controlling procedure D, E_k = K V_k/(α(R_D ∨ 1)) are compound e-variables and e-BH on them reproduces D; admissible procedures correspond to tight compound e-values. Log-optimal simple separable compound e-values are the ratio of mixture densities Σq_j/Σp_j.
14. **Randomized and closed improvements (Propositions 9.24–9.26, Theorems 9.27, 9.29, 9.32).** Stochastic rounding onto a grid preserves expectation, so rounding e-values to the e-BH grid gives a randomized e-BH with weakly more rejections; closed e-BH, built from an e-collection indexed by subsets, rejects a superset of both e-BH and minimally adaptive e-BH while keeping FDR and the post-hoc guarantee.
15. **P-merging through e-values (Lemma 11.1, Theorems 12.15, 12.17).** By multi-marginal optimal transport duality, every admissible p-merging function under arbitrary dependence is dominated by a rule that applies admissible calibrators to each p-value, averages the resulting e-values, and thresholds; this recovers Bonferroni, Simes-type, twice the mean, e times the geometric mean and log K times the harmonic mean as special cases.
16. **E-CIs and e-BY (Definition 13.1, Theorems 13.5, 13.7, 13.8).** An e-CI at level α is {θ : E(θ,α) < 1/α}; level-free families arise from stopped confidence sequences and universal inference. The e-BY procedure (reporting e-CIs at level δ|S|/K) controls the false coverage rate under any selection rule and any dependence. Majority vote of K sets each with miscoverage α has coverage 1−2α, proved by averaging the all-or-nothing e-values ϕ_k/α.
17. **Soft-rank e-value (Section 1.7, Section 6.7.7).** For statistics L_0,…,L_B exchangeable under the null and nonnegative order-preserving transforms R_b, E = (B+1)R_0/Σ_b R_b is an exact e-variable; 1/E is a soft rank dominated by the permutation p-value, and the numeraire for testing exchangeability has this form.
18. **Duality and representation (Theorems 14.2, 14.4, 14.5).** For e-variables increasing in a statistic X and p-variables decreasing in X, sup E = 1/inf P = 1/inf_P S(X;P); every e-variable built on X is dominated by a conditional expectation E_P[dQ/dP | X], and p-variables by conditional probabilities.

## Setting and assumptions
- Null P and alternative Q are arbitrary disjoint sets of probability measures on a fixed (Ω, F); no common reference measure, parametric structure or regularity is assumed in the definitional chapters (1, 2, 3, 6, 14). Chapter 5 assumes a common reference measure and iid data; Chapter 6 assumes a point alternative Q (composite alternatives handled by mixtures or plug-in, Section 3.7) and gives full proofs only for finite nulls; Chapter 7 is discrete time with a general filtration.
- Randomization: tests may be randomized (Definition 1.2); assumption (U), existence of an independent uniform under every P and Q, is used for p-variable constructions (Theorem 3.9) and randomized procedures (Sections 2.4, 9.5, 12.4); atomless probability spaces are assumed for calibrator and merging characterisations (Appendix A.1).
- Optional stopping and continuation are allowed throughout Chapter 7 (e-processes are valid at every stopping time, and continuation by a fresh e-process at a stopping time is valid, Proposition 7.9) and motivate Chapters 4 and 7; fixed-sample e-variables in other chapters are not anytime-valid unless embedded in an e-process (time mixture, Proposition 7.23).
- Multiple testing (Chapters 8, 9, 11–13) makes no assumption on dependence among e-values or p-values; PRDS and independence appear only when comparing with BH (Section 9.1.4).
- Optimality criterion is e-power, not power; the book states that log-optimal e-variables plugged into power-oriented procedures may disappoint and that the criterion must be aligned with the downstream procedure (Section 1.2.2).

## Open questions / gaps
- The authors state that sequential hypothesis testing beyond Chapter 7 and estimation via confidence sequences are deliberately out of scope, that optimality theory in the sequential setting is less well understood, and that "many fundamental questions related to filtrations remain open" [ramdasHypothesisTestingEvalues2025, Preface].
- Existence of the numeraire with no assumptions (Theorem 6.4) and the general strong duality (Theorem 6.13) are stated without proof; proofs are in Larsson, Ramdas and Ruf 2025a. The equivalence of the two e-process definitions (Definition 7.3) is likewise cited to Ramdas, Ruf, Larsson and Koolen 2020.
- Relationship between the minimum KL in Theorem 6.13 and the KL_inf quantity of the bandit literature is described as not fully worked out (Chapter 6 note).
- Numeraire e-variables are noted to be logically incoherent in general, unlike universal inference (Chapter 6 note, citing Bickel 2024); no resolution is offered.
- Composite alternatives are handled only asymptotically (mixture, plug-in, REGROW-type criteria); finite-sample optimality for composite Q is not treated.
- E-power can be infinite or −∞ in ways that misrepresent practical power (Examples 3.16, 7.25, 7.26); the book flags this but offers no alternative criterion.
- Tightness of Ville's inequality and continuous-time results are explicitly out of scope (Section 7.4).
