# Multiplicity and flexibility in clinical trials

Medical University of Vienna. Half review, half new result: how to combine unplanned interim adaptations with multiple testing, and when the unequal weighting of stages that flexible tests impose can be avoided [brannathMultiplicityFlexibilityClinical2007].

## Summary
The paper separates flexibility from adaptivity. A design with prespecified adaptivity fixes in advance how interim data determine the rest of the trial; a flexible design controls the (multiple) type I error after adaptations whose rule was never laid down, which is what combined phase IIb/III trials need because the decision to carry a treatment forward depends on efficacy, safety and external information. All flexible designs rest on what the authors name the conditional invariance principle: transform the second-stage statistic into a p-value q whose conditional null distribution given the interim data and the chosen second-stage design is uniform, so that the joint null law of (interim data, q) does not depend on the unknown adaptation rule, and fix a level-α rejection region in those terms. The conditional error function approach, combination tests, and the Müller–Schäfer conditional rejection probability principle are three ways of specifying that region. For several hypotheses one applies the closed testing principle with a flexible test for every intersection hypothesis. The new part (Secs 3–4) starts from the objection that flexible tests weight patients from different stages unequally. For two treatments against a common control it determines, for the hierarchical test and for the step-down Dunnett test, at which interim results one may drop a treatment, possibly change the sample size, and analyse the selected treatment with the conventional level-α z-test: exactly where the conditional type I error rate of the new test is no larger than the conditional type I error rates of the initial design for the selected hypothesis and for the intersection.

## Main results
- **Conditional invariance principle (Sec. 2.1).** Under an elementary null H, transform the second-stage statistic T_2 to a p-value q that is uniform conditionally on the interim data and the second-stage design. Then q is "usually" independent of the first stage, for which Liu, Proschan and Pledger 2002 is "the currently most rigorous verification"; the joint null law of interim data and q is known and invariant to the adaptation rule; any level-α region in those terms has type I error α. With nuisance parameters the region is specified through a pivotal first-stage statistic and q.
- **Conditional error function approach (Sec. 2.2.1).** Prespecify 0 ≤ A(interim data) ≤ 1 with E_H[A] ≤ α, pivotal in the sense that its maximum expectation under H can be determined; reject at stage 2 iff q ≤ A. Then P_H(reject) = P_H(q ≤ A) ≤ E_H[A] ≤ α whatever the adaptation rule. A = 1 is interim rejection, A = 0 interim acceptance.
- **Combination tests (Sec. 2.2.2).** Conditional error functions A(p) of a first-stage p-value (Proschan and Hunsberger 1995) and combination tests of p and q (Bauer 1989; Bauer and Köhne 1994) are "two different ways for specifying a rejection region in the two-dimensional (p, q)-plane" and so equivalent (Posch and Bauer 1999; Wassmer 1999); for intersection hypotheses A may depend on two or more pivotal first-stage statistics.
- **Starting from a conventional design (Sec. 2.2.3).** For any level-α test φ (fixed-sample or group-sequential), A = E_H(φ | interim data); after an unforeseen change choose a new test φ* with A* = E_H(φ* | interim data) ≤ A; if no change is needed, complete the design as planned. The overall level is bounded by E_H[A] = E_H[φ] ≤ α (Müller and Schäfer 2001, 2004). Computing A is hard with nuisance parameters (Posch et al. 2004; Timmesfeld, Schäfer and Müller 2006).
- **Flexible closed tests (Sec. 2.3).** For hypotheses H_1, …, H_k prespecify a conditional error function A_J with E_{H_J}[A_J] ≤ α for every intersection H_J; H_i is rejected iff every H_J with i ∈ J is rejected by its flexible test q_J ≤ A_J, which controls the familywise error rate strongly. At the interim one may continue with a subset I_2; hypotheses not selected are accepted; for J not contained in I_2 the second-stage p-value q_{J∩I_2} is used, valid because H_J implies H_{J∩I_2}. Two treatments: continuing with treatment 1 only, H_1 is rejected iff q_1 ≤ min(A_1, A_{1,2}). The A_J may all be the same function of the first-stage p-value p_J (A_J = c/p_J gives Fisher's combination test for every intersection; Bauer and Kieser 1999), or conditional rejection probabilities of a prespecified multiple test, e.g. for a fixed-sample Dunnett test with known variance: A_i = 1 − Φ((√n z_{1−α} − √n_1 z_i^{(1)})/√(n − n_1)) (eq. 1) and A_{1,2} = 1 − ∫ Π_{i=1,2} Φ(d√(2n/(n − n_1)) − √(2n_1/(n − n_1)) z_i^{(1)} + x) φ(x) dx (eq. 2), d the Dunnett boundary (Koenig, Brannath, Bretz and Posch, then submitted). Extensions: interim rejection of H_i iff A_J = 1 for all J ∋ i; adding hypotheses at the interim (Hommel 2001).
- **Switching to a conventional test (Sec. 3).** Known-variance normal model, n per group planned, selected treatment s, new total sample size n*. With A*_s the conditional type I error rate of the level-α z-test at n* (eq. 1 with n* for n), the conventional z-test may be used whenever A*_s ≤ min(A_s, A_{1,2}), "since decreasing the conditional type I error rates cannot inflate the overall type I error rate (but may deflate it)". Figures 1 and 2 map the admissible interim z-scores for interim looks at n/4 and n/2 and four second-stage sample sizes (decrease, as planned, reallocation of the dropped arm, increase).
- **Hierarchical test (Sec. 3.1).** Test H_2 (higher dose) first, then H_1; A_{1,2} = A_2. The lower dose can always be dropped. Without sample-size change the higher dose can be dropped and the lower tested directly iff z_1^{(1)} ≤ z_2^{(1)}, a switch "from a less to a more conservative gate-keeping test", independent of the interim timing. A decrease in sample size is admissible only for z_1^{(1)} below a threshold; an increase only when both interim effects are large, because the new test down-weights promising first-stage data. These are the sample points identified in Posch, Bauer and Brannath 2003 for a single comparison.
- **Step-down Dunnett test (Sec. 3.2).** Two opposing effects when one treatment is selected: (i) the z-test boundary z_{1−α} is below the Dunnett boundary d, which raises the conditional error for H_{1,2}; (ii) the chance of rejecting with the dropped treatment is lost, which lowers it. For two small, similar interim effects (i) dominates and the switch is not admissible without some gap to the better treatment; for two large effects (ii) dominates. With an increased sample size the better treatment can be selected if its interim effect is large enough, irrespective of the dropped one.
- **Numerical example (Sec. 4).** Modelled on an eniporide trial: n = 400 per group, hierarchical z-tests at one-sided 0.025, unblinded safety look at n_1 = 100, higher dose stopped for toxicity, its 300 unused patients reallocated so that dose 1 and placebo reach n* = 550. With z_1^{(1)} = 1.1 and z_2^{(1)} = 1.2: A*_1 = 0.0496 ≤ min(A_1, A_2) = min(0.0518, 0.0582), so the conventional z-test for dose 1 is admissible.
- **Flexibility versus prespecified adaptivity (Sec. 5).** Analysing fully flexible designs by conventional tests ("one patient one vote") is limited to adaptations that are "rather the exception than the rule"; increasing the sample size at moderate or small interim effects requires a flexible test. Arguing from a specific selection rule can mislead: always continuing with the inferior treatment at the planned sample size cannot inflate the error, yet with the Dunnett test switching to the slightly inferior treatment only when both effects are similar and small, and otherwise keeping both, does, because one switches exactly where the chance to reject is higher.

## Setting and assumptions
- Nulls: one-sided H_i: μ_i ≤ μ_0 in a homoscedastic normal model with known variance for Secs 3–4; the review part is general, with pivotality of A assumed for composite nulls.
- Two stages with a single adaptive interim look; the adaptation rule is not prespecified and may use external information.
- Error criterion: familywise error rate in the strong sense, by closed testing.
- The conditional statements are used pointwise at the observed interim values; the measure-theoretic footing is delegated to [liuUnifiedTheoryTwoStage2002](liuUnifiedTheoryTwoStage2002.md) and later revisited in [brannathProbabilisticFoundationConfirmatory2012](brannathProbabilisticFoundationConfirmatory2012.md).
