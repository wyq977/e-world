# Beyond Neyman–Pearson: e-values enable hypothesis testing with a data-driven alpha

PNAS Direct Submission, received 6 Feb 2023, accepted 21 Aug 2024, published 20 Sep 2024. Single-author. The companion paper is Grünwald 2023, "The e-posterior" (Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A), aimed at a Bayesian readership; this one is aimed at a general scientific readership and carries the decision-theoretic theorem. All proofs and several counterexamples are in the SI Appendix, which the text layer here does not include.

## Summary
The paper asks what the decision-theoretic meaning of "P ≪ α" is in Neyman–Pearson (NP) testing and answers: none, unless the loss function is binary and fixed independently of the data (the "BIND" assumption) [grunwaldBeyondNeymanPearsonEvalues2024, Intro]. Following Wald 1939, Type-I error probability is replaced by Type-I risk, the null expectation of a nonnegative loss L_b(0, a) over an action set A_b, where the index b of the loss actually faced is the outcome of a random variable B whose dependence on the data is unknowable (the "post hoc" setting). A decision rule is Type-I risk safe if sup_{P0 ∈ H(0)} E_{P0}[sup_b L_b(0, δ_b(Y))] ≤ ℓ (eq. 11–12, ℓ = 1 wlog). Proposition 1 observes that the Type-I risk safe rules are exactly those "compatible" with some [e-variable](../concepts/e-variable.md) S, i.e. L_b(0, δ_b(y)) ≤ S(y) for all y, b; the maximally compatible rule "play the largest a with L_b(0, a) ≤ S(y)" (eq. 7, eq. 15) is the e-analogue of "reject iff P · L(0,1) ≤ ℓ" with P replaced by 1/S. The main result, Theorem 1, is a complete-class-type theorem: once Type-II risk is taken into account via a Pareto notion of admissibility, every admissible rule is maximally compatible with some e-variable, and conversely under three mild conditions (mutual absolute continuity of the null, sharpness of S, richness of the problem relative to S). Section 3 repeats the exercise for confidence intervals, with loss b · 1{θ ∉ [θ_L, θ_R]} and data-dependent b: e-confidence sets built from an e-collection {S_θ} remain Type-I risk safe, whereas standard CIs, confidence distributions and objective Bayes posteriors can have infinite risk; the e-posterior 1/S_θ(Y) is the graphical summary of all e-CIs at once. For the Gaussian location family the e-CI is about 1.4 times wider than the standard one at α = 0.05 (2.72/√n vs 1.96/√n) but is valid at unknown stopping times. Sections 4–5 review the state of e-value development, list the challenges (no efficient GRO e-values for GLMs or Cox regression without model-X; universal inference is computable but underpowered in regression), and reinterpret the "roving α" α̂ := ℓ/S(y) as a roving bound ℓ/α̂ on the Type-I loss one may incur, a "quasi-conditional" paradigm between Bayes and NP [grunwaldBeyondNeymanPearsonEvalues2024, Sec. 5.1].

## Main results
- **BIND failure for p-values (Sec. 1.1.1, eq. 4–5)**: with four actions and Type-I losses 0, 20ℓ, 100ℓ, 500ℓ, the naive rule "largest a with P · L(0,a) ≤ ℓ" has Type-I risk 2.6ℓ under a uniform P; with more actions the risk can go to ∞ (SI). Multiplying L(0,a) by the probability that a is taken does not fix it.
- **Example 1 (post hoc losses)**: three two-action losses L_1, L_2, L_3 with L_b(0,b) = 20ℓ, 100ℓ, 500ℓ presented when P > 0.02, 0.001 < P ≤ 0.02, P ≤ 0.001 respectively; the naive p-rule has Type-I risk 3ℓ. Even binary losses become unsafe when the choice of loss depends on the data.
- **Eq. 7 (maximally compatible rule)**: select the largest a with L_b(0, a) ≤ S(y) · ℓ. For two actions this is the p-rule with P ↦ 1/S. Type-I risk safety holds for any e-variable S fixed before the data, any number of actions and any process generating B.
- **Example 2 (NP e-variable)**: S_np(α) := 1/α if P ≤ α, else 0, is an e-variable reproducing any level-α NP test through eq. 7, so nothing is lost by replacing P by an e-value; but S_np(α) can select at most two actions, whereas the likelihood ratio S_lr = p_1/p_0 (eq. 8–9) selects actions graded by evidence, at the price of a larger threshold per action [grunwaldBeyondNeymanPearsonEvalues2024, Sec. 1.2].
- **Definition 1 (GNP testing problem)**: a tuple (H(0), B, {(A_b, L_b(0,·)) : b ∈ B}) with Type-I losses only; a decision rule is a family {δ_b : Y → A_b}. Compatibility with S: L_b(0, δ_b(y)) ≤ S(y) (eq. 10). Type-I risk safety: existence of U with sup_b L_b(0, δ_b(y)) ≤ U(y) and sup_{P0} E_{P0}[U] ≤ 1 (eq. 12), the dominating U avoiding undefined expectations for pathological B.
- **Proposition 1**: (1) compatibility with an e-variable implies Type-I risk safety; (2) a Type-I risk safe δ is compatible with the e-variable S = U, in standard cases S(y) = sup_b L_b(0, δ_b(y)). So "Type-I risk safe" = "compatible with some e-variable" [grunwaldBeyondNeymanPearsonEvalues2024, Sec. 2.1.1].
- **Type-II risk without Type-II losses (Sec. 2.2)**: after removing actions dominated in both losses, Type-I loss increasing in a implies Type-II loss decreasing, so δ° is "Type-II strictly better" than δ iff P(L_b(0, δ°_b) < L_b(0, δ_b)) = 0 for all b, P ∈ H(0), with strict inequality somewhere (eq. 13–14). Note the comparison is under the null only. Admissible = Type-I risk safe and not Type-II strictly dominated by a Type-I risk safe rule.
- **Theorem 1 (main result)**: (1) every admissible δ is maximally compatible with some e-variable S; (2) conversely, a δ maximally compatible with S is admissible if (a) all P ∈ H(0) are mutually absolutely continuous, (b) S is sharp (E_{P0}[S] = 1 for some P0 ∈ H(0)), (c) the problem is rich relative to S (every value in the codomain of S is attained by some L_b(0, a)). Any problem can be made rich for a given sharp S by adding one loss function (SI). Squared-error loss L(0,a) = a² on A = R⁺ makes a problem rich relative to every e-variable [grunwaldBeyondNeymanPearsonEvalues2024, Sec. 2.2.1].
- **Eq. 15 (existence and uniqueness)**: for "simple" GNP problems (A_b finite unions of closed intervals, L_b(0,·) monotone and piecewise continuous, mutually absolutely continuous null) the rule δ*_b(y) = largest a with L_b(0,a) ≤ S(y) is maximally compatible and essentially unique (any two maximally compatible rules agree a.s. under every null).
- **Example 3**: simple vs simple with strict P monotone in S_lr, B ⊂ R⁺, L_b(0,1) = b. The S_np(α)-based rule is admissible if B ∋ 1/α; the S_lr-based rule is admissible if B = R⁺ but may fail to be if B is a strict subset. Admissibility is relative to the class of losses one may face.
- **E-confidence sets (Sec. 3, eq. for cs*_α)**: for an e-collection {S_θ : θ ∈ Θ} with S_θ an e-variable for H(θ) = {P : φ(P) = θ}, cs*_α(Y) = {θ : S_θ(Y) < 1/α} is a (1 − α)-confidence set, usually larger than the p-based one.
- **Example 4 (Gaussian location)**: X_i iid N(θ, 1); for anticipated n* and α*, set θ± with (n*/2)(θ − θ±)² = log(2/α*) (eq. 16) and S_θ = ½(p_{θ−}/p_θ + p_{θ+}/p_θ), a mixture of two UMP Bayes factors (Johnson 2013). A sufficient (and for all but the smallest n tight) condition for θ ∉ cs*_α is |θ − θ̂| ≥ √(2 log(2/α)/n) · g(c), c = (n*/n) · log(2/α)/log(2/α*), g(c) = ½(c^{1/2} + c^{−1/2}) (eq. 17). At n = n*, α = α* = 0.05 the half-width is √(2 log 40)/n ≈ 2.72/√n, a factor ≈ 1.4 over 1.96/√n (Fig. 1). This e-variable has GRO status for the z-test (Sec. 4).
- **Sec. 3.1 (e-process)**: S_θ(X^τ) is an e-variable for any stopping time τ, so (S_θ(X^n))_n is an [e-process](../concepts/e-process.md) and the e-CI is valid at unknown, random n; this is why n* is not set equal to n.
- **Sec. 3.2 (coverage as Type-I risk)**: action = interval, loss L_b(θ, [θ_L, θ_R]) = b · 1{θ ∉ [θ_L, θ_R]} (eq. 18); Type-I risk safety ∀θ: sup_{P ∈ H(θ)} E_P[sup_b b · 1{θ ∉ δ_b(Y)}] ≤ 1 (eq. 19); compatibility ∀θ: L_b(θ, δ_b(y)) ≤ S_θ(y) (eq. 20). Compatible rules are Type-I risk safe; admissible iff no compatible rule is never wider and sometimes strictly narrower. Under BIND the sup over b leaves the expectation and b = 1/α recovers standard (1 − α)-CIs (Sec. 3.2.1).
- **Example 5**: substituting α = 1/b in eq. 17 gives the admissible e-CI [θ̂ − A, θ̂ + A], A = √(2 log(2b)/n) · g(c) (eq. 21), safe irrespective of how B depends on Y.
- **Sec. 3.3 (confidence distributions are not post hoc valid)**: with the flat-prior posterior N(θ̂, 1/n) (which equals the fiducial and confidence distribution), choosing the smallest symmetric interval with posterior expected loss ≤ 1 (eq. 22–23) returns the standard (1 − 1/b)-CI; the true risk E_{P_θ*}[B(Y) · 1{θ* ∉ δ'_{B(Y)}(Y)}] (eq. 24) can be ∞ when B depends on Y (SI example, after Grünwald 2018 "Safe probability", Example 8). Sec. 3.3.1: the long-run average (eq. 25) a Neyman-style DM expects to be ≤ 1 can converge a.s. to ∞.
- **Sec. 3.4 (e-posterior)**: P̄(θ | Y) := 1/S_θ(Y), introduced in Grünwald 2023 and independently in Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (arXiv 2020, App. D5); valid for post hoc Type-I risk assessment, recommended as the summary of estimation uncertainty in place of confidence distributions.
- **Sec. 4 (state of the art)**: GRO e-methods exist for the z-test, t-test, two proportions, logrank test (deployed in a live meta-analysis), group-invariant and linear/GP regression, k-sample exponential families, CMH, model-X conditional independence; CI widths O(f(n)/√n) with f from constant to O(√log n). Sec. 4.1: GRO computation is inefficient in general; hard cases are nonlinear regression (GLMs, Cox) without model-X; universal inference is computable but not power-competitive in medium/high dimension (Tse and Davison 2022).
- **Sec. 5.1 (roving α)**: a fully conditional statement (eq. 26, "among studies with α̂ ≤ α_0 we err at most a fraction α_0") is correct only for a trusted Bayesian posterior and false for α̂ = P or 1/S. Setting α̂ := ℓ/S(y) is correct when read as a roving bound ℓ/α̂ on the Type-I loss L_B(0, a) one dares to take: compatibility then gives eq. 11. B is "conditional on" data, performance is evaluated unconditionally: the quasi-conditional paradigm.
- **Sec. 5.2**: the risk bound ℓ must still be set independently of Y; a natural choice is ℓ = L_b(1, 0), the Type-II loss of "do nothing".
- **Sec. 5.3–5.6**: Martin–Liu inferential models also flag the posterior/true-coverage discrepancy but still assume BIND; the major challenge is sociological (abandoning p-values and embracing optional stopping), with the tech industry ahead; future work on quasi-conditional inference, the relation to Bates et al.'s principal-agent testing, e-values as a Fisherian evidence measure (conjectured, SI), GLM e-variables, software, tutorials. Conclusion: robustness to the decision task, not to outliers or misspecification.

## Setting and assumptions
- Null H(0) an arbitrary set of distributions on Y; no alternative is needed for Type-I risk safety, and the alternative enters only through the choice of S (Example 2, Example 4). Type-II losses are never specified; the admissibility ordering (eq. 13–14) is defined through Type-I losses under the null after pruning dominated actions.
- Loss index B may depend on the data in an unknown way; the decision-maker sees only the realised b. The risk bound ℓ is set independently of the data (Sec. 5.2) and normalised to 1 (Sec. 2.1). Losses are nonnegative with L(0,0) = 0 by convention but not necessity.
- Theorem 1 part 2 needs mutual absolute continuity of the null (boundary points excluded), sharpness of S and richness of the problem relative to S; "simple" problems (eq. 15) need monotone piecewise-continuous Type-I losses on finite unions of intervals.
- Confidence setting: scalar θ, null H(θ) = {P : φ(P) = θ}, e-collection {S_θ}; Example 4 assumes iid N(θ, 1) with known variance and uses a two-point mixture e-variable with fixed anticipated n*, α*; the stopping time τ may be unknown. The e-CI width bound (eq. 17) is "tight for all but the smallest n" (SI).
- Optional stopping and continuation are not the subject but are used: eq. 6 e-variables are stated to be valid under optional continuation [grunwaldSafeTesting2024; vovkEvaluesCalibrationCombination2021], and Sec. 3.1 uses the e-process property for random n.
- The counterexamples with infinite risk (multi-action p-rule, confidence-distribution rule) and the enlargement argument for richness are in the SI Appendix, which is not available in the text layer.

## Open questions / gaps
- Theorem 1 is stated with proofs in the SI only; the generalisation to GNP decision problems (estimation, eq. 19–20) and its admissibility theorem are also SI-only. Worth retrieving the SI if the wiki is to cite the exact regularity conditions.
- Admissibility of the maximally compatible rule depends on richness relative to S; Example 3 shows the likelihood-ratio rule may be inadmissible when B is a strict subset of R⁺. How to choose S when the class of possible losses is partly known is not addressed.
- The ordering "Type-II strictly better" is defined under the null only; the paper's claim that this captures Type-II risk rests on the pruning of dominated actions. No power or Type-II risk comparison between e-based and p-based rules under alternatives is given beyond the 1.4 CI width factor.
- Which inferential models (Martin–Liu) behave like e-posteriors (Sec. 5.3)? Open.
- Whether e-values are a better Fisherian evidence measure than p-values is conjectured, with motivation only in the SI (Sec. 5.5).
- Efficient GRO e-values for GLMs and Cox regression without model-X remain unavailable (Sec. 4.1).
