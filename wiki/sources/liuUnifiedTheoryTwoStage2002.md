# A unified theory of two-stage adaptive designs

J&J Pharmaceutical Research and Development (Liu, Pledger) and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (Proschan). A Theory and Methods paper asking when the two justifications then in circulation for adaptive designs, a conditional type I error bound and the independence of stage-wise p-values, are actually true [liuUnifiedTheoryTwoStage2002].

## Summary
Adaptive procedures had been justified in two ways: Proschan and Hunsberger 1995, Cui, Hung and Wang 1999 and Liu and Chi 2001 argued that the overall type I error is protected if the second-stage test protects the conditional type I error given the first stage; Bauer 1989, Bauer and Köhne 1994, Fisher 1998 and Lehmacher and Wassmer 1999 assumed that the second-stage p-value is independent of the first whatever the adaptation rule. The paper asks whether these claims are always true and gives a framework in which they are. Interim data are a sub-σ-field G of (Ω, F, P); an adaptation rule is a G-measurable index g with countable range M; each option m ∈ M has an outcome X_m defined on the same space, and the adapted outcome is X_g(ω) = X_{g(ω)}(ω). The Adaptation Lemma says X_g = Σ_m 1{g = m}X_m is a random variable whose conditional expectation given G is the patchwork of the E(X_m | G), so any conditional property (equality or inequality against a G-measurable C) holding for each X_m almost everywhere on {g = m} holds for X_g. From this follow the distribution of adapted statistics (Theorem 1), an if-and-only-if characterisation of independence between stages (Theorem 2), unbiased and minimum-variance adaptive estimators (Theorems 3–4), adaptive pivots and confidence intervals (Theorem 5), and the adaptive conditional test (Theorem 6), which is the conditional error function principle in general form and does not need independent p-values. Section 4 gives consistency and weak-convergence results. Section 5 shows by counterexample that measurability of g, a common null distribution of the second-stage statistics, and countability of M each matter.

## Main results
- **Adaptation Lemma and corollary (Sec. 2).** X_g = Σ_{m∈M} 1_{G_m}X_m with G_m = {g = m} ∈ G is a random variable. If E(|X_m| | G) ≤ L almost everywhere on G_m for each m and an integrable L, then X_g is integrable, E(X_g) = Σ_m E(1_{G_m}X_m), and E(X_g | G) = Σ_m 1_{G_m}E(X_m | G) with probability 1. Corollary: if every X_m satisfies (a) E(X_m | G) = C, (b) ≤ C, or (c) ≥ C almost everywhere on G_m for an integrable G-measurable C, so does X_g; for nonnegative X_m integrability of each X_m suffices for (a) and (b). The same holds for any σ-field G′ with σ(g) ⊂ G′ ⊂ F. "The requirements of M being countable, g being G measurable, and X_m satisfying a conditional property on (Ω, G, P) are crucial."
- **Distribution (Theorem 1).** The conditional law of Y_g given G is Σ_m μ_{G,m}1_{G_m}; the unconditional law is its expectation; if each Y_m is independent of G ("marginally G independent") the law of Y_g is Σ_m μ_mP(G_m), and if the Y_m share a law μ_0 then Y_g has law μ_0 conditionally and unconditionally.
- **Independence (Theorem 2).** With F_1, F_2 independent σ-fields, Z_1 and g F_1-measurable and Z_{2m} F_2-measurable: (a) (g, Z_{2g}) is independent of Z_1 iff g and Z_1 are independent; (b) (g, Z_1) is independent of Z_{2g} iff the Z_{2m} have the same distribution for all m with P(G_m) ≠ 0. Since "for practical applications, measurability and countability hold true for any adaptation rule", (b) establishes the validity of the procedures of Bauer 1989, Bauer and Köhne 1994, Lehmacher and Wassmer 1999 and Fisher 1998 for continuous distributions. Example 1: Stein's 1945 two-stage t-test, where g depends only on the first-stage variance estimate and the adapted z-statistic is standard normal and independent of it. Example 2: Proschan and Hunsberger's design with the first-stage t-statistic transformed to Z_1 = Φ^{−1}(F_1(T_1)) and Z_{2m} likewise, so that any size-α region in the (Z_1, Z_{2g}) plane is a valid test.
- **Point estimation (Theorems 3–4).** If the θ̂_m are unbiased, uniformly bounded in L¹ (or nonnegative) and marginally G independent, then E(θ̂_g | G) = θ; if each θ̂_m is UMVU then θ̂_g is the uniformly minimum variance unbiased adaptive estimator among marginally G-independent estimators, with var(θ̂_g) = Σ_m var(θ̂_m)P(G_m). Example 4: when n_2 depends on the first-stage effect, the pooled mean difference wθ̂_1 + (1 − w)θ̂_{2g} with w = n_1/(n_1 + g) has bias cov(θ̂_1, w) > 0; any F_1-measurable weight uncorrelated with θ̂_1 (a constant, or one depending only on the variance estimate) removes it.
- **Confidence intervals (Theorem 5).** If the Y_m(θ) are pivots with a common law F and marginally G independent, Y_g(θ) is a pivot with law F, giving equal-tailed intervals and a median-unbiased estimator by inversion.
- **Adaptive conditional test (Theorem 6).** Let H = ∩_m H_m and let each p_m satisfy P_{H_m}(p_m ≤ C | G) ≤ C almost everywhere on G_m for any G-measurable C ∈ [0, 1]. Then P_H(p_g ≤ C | G) ≤ C and P_H(p_g ≤ C) ≤ E_H(C); if E_H(C) ≤ α, rejecting when p_g ≤ C is a level-α test, of size α when p_g is continuous and E_H(C) = α. Motivation: Fisher's exact test, where a prespecified C(m) for each total number of events m may exceed 0.05 for some m while the unconditional level stays at 0.05. Example 5: with C = C(p_1) this is Proschan and Hunsberger's conditional error function; "only independence of p_1 and p_{2m} for each m is required", which does not imply independence of p_1 and p_{2g}. Example 6: covariate selection in ANCOVA by a prespecified automated rule, with p_m from the randomisation distribution restricted to {g = m}.
- **Asymptotics (Sec. 4, Theorems 7–9).** If second-stage estimators are consistent with a uniform tail bound and g_{n_1} → ∞ in probability, θ̂_{2g} is consistent, so the naive weighted estimator of Example 4 is consistent. If first- and second-stage statistics converge weakly and g_{n_1} → ∞, they are asymptotically independent with the product limit (Theorem 8; Example 8, variance-based sample-size re-estimation under local alternatives, where the t-statistic keeps the planned power). For a rule converging in distribution, the limit of (X, g, Y_g) is obtained by adapting the limiting variables (Theorem 9; Example 9, choosing at the interim the endpoint with the larger z-statistic).
- **Outside the framework (Sec. 5).** Example 10: a sample-size function n_2(p_1) = m on E_m, where (0, 1] = ∪_m E_m is a countable union of disjoint translates of a non-measurable set, has conditional level 0.05 at every p_1 yet no well-defined type I error; "one must assume that the human brain acts in a measurable way". Example 11: switching between a rank-sum and a t-test at stage 2 according to p_1 gives second-stage p-values with different null laws (discrete and continuous uniform), so Theorem 2 fails and p_1, p_{2g} are dependent, because the smallest attainable rank-sum p-value has conditional probability zero on one branch only. Example 12: uncountable M. On [0, 1] with Lebesgue measure let Z_1(ω) = ω, U uniform and independent of Z_1, Z_{2s} = U·1{Z_1 ≠ s}, g = Z_1; each Z_{2s} is uniform and the family is independent of Z_1, but Z_{2g} ≡ 0, so Fisher's combination test on (Z_1, Z_{2g}) always rejects.
- **Discussion (Sec. 6).** The theory "enables a fundamental paradigmatic shift", but adaptation is not a substitute for planning; more options mean more chances of a wrong decision; "the validity of adaptation lies in the validity of the method to which it adapts". The theory covers changes of analysis, population, treatment arms and allocation ratio, and is offered as a basis for adaptive group-sequential designs.

## Setting and assumptions
- One probability space (Ω, F, P) carrying the interim σ-field G, the rule g and every potential outcome X_m; g is G-measurable with countable range.
- Conditional properties are required almost everywhere on {g = m}, not pointwise.
- Two stages; for Theorem 2 the stage σ-fields are independent.
- Null hypotheses H_m may differ by option, with H their intersection; nuisance parameters are handled by ancillarity (Example 1) or transformation to pivots.
- Proofs of most theorems are in a technical report; the appendix proves Theorems 2, 8 and 9.

## Relation to other sources
- [brannathProbabilisticFoundationConfirmatory2012](brannathProbabilisticFoundationConfirmatory2012.md): answers this paper. It grants that countable M with the bound holding for almost all x′ ∈ {g = m} makes every version of the conditional probability work, but objects that {g = m} is unknown when the rule is not prespecified and that the almost-everywhere condition excludes adaptations justified only at the observed interim value; it replaces the conditions with a regression model and re-examines Example 10, showing the rejection event can be given probability 0.05. This paper's claim that measurability and countability "hold true for any adaptation rule" in practice is the point of disagreement.
- [brannathMultiplicityFlexibilityClinical2007](brannathMultiplicityFlexibilityClinical2007.md): calls this paper "the currently most rigorous verification" that an invariant conditional null distribution implies independence from the first stage.
- [poschAdaptiveTwoStage1999](poschAdaptiveTwoStage1999.md): the designs whose validity Theorems 2 and 6 establish, in one notation.
- [brannathAnytimevalidTestingEvalues2026a](brannathAnytimevalidTestingEvalues2026a.md): the hypothesis of Theorem 6, P(p_m ≤ C | G) ≤ C for G-measurable C, is the two-stage form of the sequential "p-clud" condition there. Wiki's reading, not the paper's wording: the corollary with nonnegative X_m and C = 1 says that a measurable choice among countably many conditional e-variables is a conditional e-variable, which is the step behind [optional continuation](../concepts/optional-continuation.md) with a data-chosen next study; the e-value literature in this wiki gets the same conclusion by fixing a filtration and adapted processes in advance.
- [koningAnytimeValidityFree2026](koningAnytimeValidityFree2026.md): the Fisher's-exact-test motivation of Theorem 6 (a prespecified conditional level C(m)) is a conditional significance level in that paper's sense.
- Not sources: Proschan and Hunsberger 1995; Cui, Hung and Wang 1999; Liu and Chi 2001; Bauer 1989; Bauer and Köhne 1994; Fisher 1998; Lehmacher and Wassmer 1999; Stein 1945; Hogg 1982 (the definition of adaptation); Wittes and Brittain 1990; Gould 1992; Gould and Shih 1992; Wassmer 1998; Billingsley 1986.
