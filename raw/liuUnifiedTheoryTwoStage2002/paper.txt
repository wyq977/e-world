Qing Liu, Michael A. PROSCHAN, and Gordon W. PLEDGER
Adaptation of a clinical trial occurs when there is a change from the planned course of action in design, sample size, or method of analysis. There are many proposed adaptations in the literature, but no comprehensive mathematical theory to support them. The standard Neyman-Pearson theory is not suitable for the setting, and its use may result in invalid inference. This article provides a general formulation of adaptation, under which its validity can be broadly and rigorously established. The basic theory on distribution, stochastic independence, point estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and asymptotics is developed. Various examples are given to illustrate applications of the theory. The goal is to provide a solid theoretical foundation to aid the development of adaptive methods for clinical investigations.
KEY WORDS: Adaptive design; Adaptive p value; Conditional error function; Confidence intervals; Pivotal quantity; Two-stage adaptive design; Type I error rate; Unbiased estimator.
1. BACKGROUND
Adaptation is an integral part of scientific investigations. Early examples in statistical literature include changing the originally planned sample size in a t test setting when a subsample reveals that the variance has been misguessed a priori (Stein 1945), and estimating location parameters using trimmed samples arising from symmetric distributions (Takeuchi 1971; Johns 1974; Cox and Hinkley 1974, p. 350). More recently, there has been a resurgence of interest in adaptive methods in clinical investigations of treatment regimens. Pilot, or phase 2, trials are often conducted before initiation of larger-scale, confirmative phase 3 trials. But phase 2 trials are often dissimilar enough to the phase 3 trials so as to not give reliable information on the most appropriate design, sample size, or method of statistical analysis for the phase 3 trials. This has led to the development of methods that use the internal pilot (i.e., part of the phase 3 trial data) as the basis of adaptations. A change in sample size might be based on an estimate of a nuisance parameter, such as the variance for a normally distributed outcome or the overall probability of an event for a binary outcome, such as death (e.g., Wittes and Brittain 1990, Gould 1992; Gould and Shih 1992). Through simulation studies under specified settings, these procedures are shown to not materially inflate the type I error rate. To allow wider flexibility in adaptation (e.g., change of sample size, study dose, endpoints, methods of analysis), many authors have proposed procedures using observed treatment effects. There appear to be two different justifications for these procedures, which are best illustrated for a two-stage adaptive design in which the data from both stages are independent and the first data are used for adaptation. Proschan and Hunsberger (1995), Cui, Hung, and Wang (1999), and Liu and Chi (2001) essentially stated that an adaptive test procedure protects the overall type I error rate as long as the conditional test at the second stage given the first-stage data protects the conditional type I error rate. These are not simply academic questions. Changes in the design of clinical trials are commonly made, although only very recently has the need for adjusted p values been recognized. General methods are provided for point estimation, confidence intervals, and overall p values, which (except in Liu and Chi 2001) have not been addressed much in the literature. Asymptotic theory is also developed for adaptive methods. Examples are given to demonstrate a wide variety of applications that the theory encompasses. 3.5 Adaptive Conditional Test
In nonadaptive testing situations, if there exists a random variable p whose null distribution is stochastically at least as large as the continuous uniform distribution on [0, 1], then the null hypothesis H can be rejected at level a if p < a. Typically, the random variable p is called a "p value". We extend the notion to conditional tests.
We motivate the development using the familiar example of Fisher's exact test for comparing two binomial proportions ir1 and 7r2. The sample sizes n and n2 in the two groups are fixed in advance; however, the total number of events, g, is random. Conditional on a realization of the total number of
events, say m, the number of treatment events has a noncentral hypergeometric distribution (Johnson and Kotz 1969, p. 157). The null hypothesis of no treatment effect (i.e., rT- = rr2) implies, for each m, the null hypothesis Hm, that the noncentrality parameter, logit(7l-) - logit((72), of the hypergeometric distribution with total m is 0. 5. ADAPTATIONS BEYOND THE CURRENT FRAMEWORK
Part of the appeal of adaptive methods proposed in the literature is their great flexibility. But rigorous justification becomes difficult, if not impossible, if the adaptations lie outside the framework of this article, as we now show in the context of two-stage procedures. One necessary but not always explicitly stated assumption is that g be measurable. Although a technicality, measurability of g is important for adaptation theory, as demonstrated in the following example.
Example 10. For the two-stage adaptive design for sample size adjustment, Proschan and Hunsberger (1995) stated that the additional sample size n2 can be determined in a subjective manner by considering conditional power given the firststage data. Thus one need only decide the additional sample size for the p value p, actually observed rather than specifying in advance a measurable function n2(Pl). 6. DISCUSSION
The general theory of adaptation developed in this article not only justifies the existing practice of adaptation, but also extends its validity. More importantly, it enables a fundamental paradigmatic shift in clinical investigations, encouraging proactively adaptive planning, monitoring, and decision making. No longer must a clinical trial design be caught in the dilemma between uncertainty and specificity in protocol designs. The added flexibility of adaptation is not without potential pitfalls, however. Obviously, adaptation should never be used as a crutch to avoid careful planning and thorough research of prior studies in initial trial designs. A less obvious but crucial point is that there is a delicate balance in terms of the number of possible adaptations to allow. The larger this number, the more the possibilities of wrong decisions-choosing the wrong endpoint, subgroup, and so on. The smaller this number, the greater the likelihood of eliminating potentially useful adaptations. In addition, consistency across trials must be kept in mind, because substantially different adaptations reduce the overall credibility. Although planning for various types of adaptation is critical, it is difficult to specify a definitive adaptation rule. As Canner (1981) stated, "decision-making in clinical trials is complicated and often protracted...no single statistical decision rule or procedure can take the place of well-reasoned consideration of all aspects of the data by a group of concerned, competent and experienced persons with a wide range of scientific backgrounds and points of view." It should also be emphasized that the validity of adaptation lies in the validity of the method to which it adapts. Although the Neyman-Pearson theory is not suitable for the adaptive setting, it does provide the fundamental building blocks for a valid adaptive procedure. We hope that the general adaptation theory will guide the development of new and more efficient methods, shifting the focus from validity to sensitivity. Although this article has focused on sample size modifications, the theory covers other types of adaptations, including changing the primary analysis or patient population, dropping or adding treatment groups, and changing the randomization allocation ratio. It could also be the basis for a unified framework for adaptive group sequential designs. Indeed, as Hogg (1982) envisioned, "the future seems bright for adaptive methods, and these will bring application and theory closer together."
