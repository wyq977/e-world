Adaptive Two Stage Designs and the Conditional Error Function
Martin Posch and Peter Bauer
Department of Medical Statistics University of Vienna Austria
Summary
Proschan and Hunsberger (1995) suggest the use of a conditional error function to construct a two stage test that meets the a level and allows a very flexible reassessment of the sample size after the interim analysis. In this note we show that several adaptive designs can be formulated in terms of such an error function. The conditional power function defined similarly provides a simple method for sample size reassessment in adaptive two stage designs.
Key words: Adaptive two stage designs; Conditional error function; Stopping rule.
1. Introduction
Several adaptive two stage designs with stopping rules which allow a very flexible reassessment of sample size at the second stage have been proposed recently (Bauer and Ko» hne, 1994; Proschan and Hunsberger, 1995; Shen and Fisher, 1999; Lehmacher and Wassmer, 1999). For simplicity let us consider a one sided null hypothesis H0 : m à 0 versus the alternative H1 : m > 0, for the mean of a normal distribution with known variance.

6. Comparison of the Conditional Error Functions
Figure 1 shows three types of conditional error functions with a à 0:025 and a1 à 0:0147 which correspond to the PH design, the LW design with Pocock-type critical limits, and the BK design with a2 à a. All three designs have been chosen in such a way that for z1 > z1ˇa1 the conditional error function is 1: then the null hypothesis is rejected already at the first stage. The SF conditional error function is omitted since it does not allow early rejection.
The LW design does not allow early acceptance. Hence, even for negative z1 there is a small probability to reject H0 after the second stage. The PH and BK designs for small z1 do not lead to a second stage because of early acceptance. Note that the conditional error functions of these designs lead to different values of a0: the circular error function for the PH design leads to an a0 of 0:174, the BK design to a0 à 0:221. Note that Figure 1 also demonstrates for n2 ! 1 how large the critical level for the test statistics in the pooled sample could become in the worst case. When proceeding to the second stage this `limiting level' is given by the value of AÖz1Ü in the point z1ˇa1. It is largest for the PH and lowest for the LW design.

8. Applications
The application of the procedures is straight forward and has been demonstrated in the original papers. At the first stage the acceptance region is z1 < z1ˇa0. Not all the approaches have been studied for a0 < 1. In case of unknown variance the PH approach is valid only asymptotically. The BK approach is exact whenever p1 and p2 are p-values of the corresponding one sided t-test calculated from the disjoint samples at the first and second stage respectively. The other two approaches are exact in the case of unknown variance if
Z à w1Fˇ1Ö1 ˇ p1Ü á w2Fˇ1Ö1 ˇ p2Ü (with their respective weights) is used as the final test statistic. This modification exploits the fact that the p-values are uniformly distributed under H0. The statistic Z is tested at full level a in the SF approach. In the LW design an adjusted local level a2Ö<aÜ is applied because of the possibility of early rejection.
9. Concluding Remarks
It has been shown that different approaches for adaptive two stage designs can be characterized by specific conditional error functions. Proschan and Hunsberger (1995) use the same test statistics as the uniformly most powerful test in the joint sample would use. However, the critical regions have to be adjusted for the flexibility. The other approaches use a function of the separate test statistics in the samples at the two stages. Shen and Fisher pay the price for the flexibility by using pre-chosen weights for both stages. Lehmacher and Wassmer use an unweighted analysis over the two stages. As they point out, their approach can be reduced to the more flexible concept of combining p-values from two separate tests performed at the two stages. This allows an application under more general distributional assumptions, e.g., unknown variability in the normal case or for other distributions as it is possible in the BK design. For all designs we showed that they may result in a final test with a local level exceeding a. Wassmer (1998) compared the BK approach with the PH design and found very similar behavior with respect to power. Little is known on the ``optimal∫ choice of the conditional error function. It has to be expected that optimal proposals will depend on the underlying assumptions in the design phase. If, e.g., in the planning phase the unknown variability is underestimated then particular designs with early acceptance (a0 < 1) may perform poorly (Posch and Bauer, 1999).
