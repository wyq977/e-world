W. Brannath, G. Gutjahr, and P. Bauer
Adaptive designs allow the investigator of a confirmatory trial to react to unforeseen developments by changing the design. This broad flexibility comes at the price of a complex statistical model where important components, such as the adaptation rule, remain unspecified. It has thus been doubted whether Type I error control can be guaranteed in general adaptive designs. This criticism is fully justified as long as the probabilistic framework on which an adaptive design is based remains vague and implicit. Therefore, an indispensable step lies in the clarification of the probabilistic fundamentals of adaptive testing. We demonstrate that the two main principles of adaptive designs, namely the conditional Type I error rate and the conditional invariance principle, will provide Type I error rate control, if the conditional distribution of the second-stage data, given the first-stage data, can be described in terms of a regression model. A similar assumption is required for regression analysis where the distribution of the covariates is a nuisance parameter and the model needs to be identifiable independently from the covariate distribution. We further show that under the assumption of a regression model, the events of an arbitrary adaptive design can be embedded into a formal probability space without the need of posing any restrictions on the adaptation rule. As a consequence of our results, artificial constraints that had to be imposed on the investigator only for mathematical tractability of the model are no longer
necessary.
KEY WORDS: Confirmatory clinical trial; Conditional error function; Conditional power; Conditional rejection probability; Combination test; Sample size reassessment.
1. INTRODUCTION
Completely prespecifymg all aspects of a confirmatory trial is desirable but not always possible. Frequently, ethical or eco
nomical considerations demand modification of design char
acteristics, such as sample sizes, study doses, endpoints, and follow-up times, after reviewing interim results from a running trial. Bauer (1989) introduced the name adaptive design to de scribe such data-dependent selection of the final design of a trial
during an interim analysis.
Conceptually, a confirmatory trial with adaptive design con sists of two stages: the design of the first stage is fixed, while the
design of the second stage can be changed (or chosen) based on results from an interim analysis performed at the end of the first
stage. Two approaches have been suggested to control the Type I error rate in a confirmatory adaptive trial: on the one hand, the
conditional Type I error principle of Proschan and Hunsberger (1995) and Müller and Schäfer (2001, 2004), and on the other
hand, the conditional invariance principle (Brannath, König, and Bauer 2007), a special case of which are combination tests
(Bauer 1989; Bauer and Köhne 1994; Brannath, Posch, and Bauer 2002).
Although both approaches are intuitively convincing, their
general validity has been doubted for two reasons. The first con cern points to the technical issue that with both principles, the re
jection regions are defined via conditional probabilities. To give
conditional probabilities a clear meaning, we need to choose a
version and, in general, there exist uncountably many different versions. In Section 3, we will present an example which shows
that without fixing the version in advance, the application of the
conditional Type I error rate or the conditional invariance princi
ple permits arbitrary Type I error inflations. Although the choice of a version is relevant for any type of adaptations, it appears
most relevant for adaptations that influence the correlation (or
common distribution) between the first- and the second-stage
data. An illustrative theoretical example of this type will be
given in Section 3. Other examples are trials with survival data,
where sample sizes or event numbers are changed (Schäfer and Müller 2001; Bauer and Posch 2004; Wassmer 2006), oncol
ogy trials with data-dependent subgroup enrichment (Brannath et al. 2009), and designs with repeated measurements (Yin and Shen 2005; Götte, Hommel, and Faldum 2009). In these cases, additional assumptions are required to permit the determination
of a unique and valid version for the conditional distribution of
the second-stage data, which applies for any (unknown) adapta tion rule. The second concern points to a more fundamental and
completely unresolved issue: it is not clear whether any adaptive
trial can be modeled on a probability space.
Regarding the first-mentioned concern, Liu, Proschan, and
Pledger (2002) and Liu and Pledger (2006) showed Type I er ror control under the assumption that the adaptive trial can be modeled on a probability space and the design is selected by a
measurable rule from a set that is at most countable. Further
more, Liu, Proschan, and Pledger (2002) and Liu and Pledger (2006) considered a somewhat stronger formulation of the con
ditional Type I error rate principle than usually imposed. We will see that the stronger formulation does not cover impor
tant applications. We will furthermore illustrate by a counterex
ample that the more common (weaker) principle requires that
with each adaptation, a uniquely prespecified version for con ditional probabilities is associated. Otherwise, arbitrary Type

designs.

7. DISCUSSION
The present work has clarified basic issues underlying con
firmatory adaptive designs. In particular, two conclusions have
This content downloaded from 192.16.197.194 on Thu, 05 Nov 2026 09:43:32 UTC All use subject to https://about.jstor.org/terms


Brannath, Gutjahr, and Bauer: Probabilistic Foundation of Confirmatory Adaptive Designs
been obtained. First, we need to assume a regression model to
generally justify Type I error rate control. A regression model is justified under minimal assumptions if the first- and second
stage data are from independent cohorts. Second, we have shown
that a regression model allows us to construct a probabilistic
model for an adaptive experiment even without any countability
or measurability assumption on the design space and the adapta
tion rule. This answers questions in Liu, Proschan, and Pledger
(2002) whether adaptive designs can be justified in a mathemat ically rigorous way without making restrictive assumptions on
the design space and the adaptation rule.
Our counterexample in Section 3 and other counterexamples
in Liu, Proschan, and Pledger (2002) show that following, for the
given second-stage design, the rejection rule only up to a null set
that has not been prespecified can produce arbitrary Type I error rate inflations. Of course, similar counterexamples apply to the
classical two-sample i-test and other conventional test designs.
However, in adaptive designs, a violation of the rejection rule on a data-dependent null set can only be avoided by using a unique canonical version for the conditional distributions that
either have been prespecified or are necessarily implied by the
chosen second-stage design (and hence used by everyone).
Since the overall probability space is generally unknown in adaptive designs, this version must be identifiable without ref
erence to the overall probability space. We have seen that such
identification is possible (and presumably only possible) under
the assumption of a regression model for the second-stage data
in dependence of the interim data and the second-stage design. We have also seen that under this assumption, we neither need
a countability nor a measurability assumption for the adapta tion rule. The difficulties in finding prespecified versions of the
conditional distributions have gone largely unnoticed, presum
ably, because regression models have always been an implicit
assumption in adaptive designs, even though authors do not
always explicitly refer to regression models. We acknowledge that the regression model may determine the conditional null distribution of the second-stage data only
up to unknown parameters such as the residual variance and
the baseline hazard, which are nuisance parameters. The conditional distribution of the adapted test statistics usually also
depends on the main parameter because the null and alternative
hypotheses are one sided. In the first case, we often have a
pivotal second-stage test statistic whose conditional null distri
bution is independent from the unknown nuisance parameters
(under the regression model). In the second case, we must con sider the maximum over all possible conditional distributions.
For the conditional Type I error rate principle, this means that
the maximum of all possible conditional expectations of the test decision function must be below the prespecified conditional error function. For the conditional invariance principle, this means that all conditional null distributions must be dominated by the same prespecified conditional null distribution (e.g., the
uniform distribution). In other examples, the conditional Type
I error rate itself can depend on nuisance parameters (because
it has been specified implicitly via a prespecified conventional conditional test). In this case, the conditional error rate or the
conditional invariance principle must be applied simultaneously
for all parameter values corresponding to the null hypothesis For more details on this, we refer to Müller and Schäfer (2004)
Timmesfeld, Schäfer, and Müller (2007), Gutjahr, Brannath, and Bauer (2011), and Gutjahr, Posch, and Brannath (2011).
We finally note that Type I error control and correctness of
/?-values are only two of the many requirements in practical applications. In particular, even if it has now been shown that
the principles of confirmatory statistical inference for adaptive designs can be applied with highly complex design spaces, the
number of potential second-stage designs in a well-designed trial will usually be more restricted. Reasons for this restriction
can be difficulties with the interpretation of the results if dif
ferent hypotheses are tested during the first and second stages.
Moreover, analyzing the power of a planned trial requires as
sumptions about the possible second-stage designs.
